In the name of sustenance, which supports all labor and without which no knowledge can be sought, I set down this account of my days at the dining hall called Crossroads, so that what it means to eat at this institution might be recorded for those who come after. I write as a student of this university, a first-year in applied mathematics, housed at Foothill on the northeastern edge of the campus. I came to this country from China, my first time abroad since the pandemic closed the world's borders, and I found myself assigned by lottery to a double room with another Chinese student I had never met. This is how my life here began: not by choice, but by arrangement, like so much else in the life of a student.

I eat at Crossroads for lunch and dinner most days—it is a twenty-minute walk from my dorm, down the hill past the business and law schools, cutting across Unit 1—but I go because it offers more choices than any other hall. The building is a white square with tall ceilings and long steel bars. The entrance wall is entirely glass, but the inner walls are windowless. At its center is an open patio, so the layout forms a kind of O, and one must walk around it to reach every station. The hall belongs to Berkeley Student Services.

The entrance has two doors. One has a grey steel handle and opens by hand. The other has a button on the wall beside it, intended for wheelchairs, but used constantly by students too lazy to pull, even though the automatic door is slower. Inside, steel bar handles channel diners toward the card readers, where we swipe our student IDs. The reader emits a faint beep, and the light changes from red to yellowish green. This is the ritual of entry. Behind the counter sits a staff member on a black chair, identical to those in every dining hall on campus. They rotate and adjust their position but rarely stand. When a visitor pays with credit, the terminal must be reconfigured, and the line slows to a crawl.

The dining hall levels those who enter. Undergraduates eat beside graduate instructors and tutors who have taught them. All races, all classes sit at the same steel tables. The only distinction is the swipe of a card, and even that is not absolute: when the lane grows crowded, people slip through the exit and eat for free. On some days, homeless men and women enter because someone has bought them a meal. On others, visiting schoolchildren crowd the lane, their voices filling the hall, and the staff must process their payments one by one while the line slows to nothing.

The meal follows a fixed route. I enter and go first to the allergy counter, in the corner nearest the bathroom—not ideal, though I suspect this was not deliberate. The pots are covered, so that when I first arrived I could not identify what was offered. Masala was a meaningless sound to me, a name attached to something I could not picture. Over time I learned what I liked: mushrooms, bok choy, broccoli. I go here first because the plates are kept separate, reserved for students with allergies, and this saves me from carrying multiple trays.

From there I move to the pasta station, which has no line most days. The carrots, cut into small rounds, have a watery texture I do not enjoy—they remind me of tofu pressed to an unnatural firmness. I continue clockwise around the O, past the frying stand, and stop at the main dish station, which changes its offerings more than any other. I have eaten food from Africa, China, and Vietnam here. I take rice sometimes, green beans, and occasionally meat—though I am not a vegetarian. I simply dislike oily food, and much of the meat is prepared that way.

I stop at the sandwich counter only for the salmon salad, chopped fine, neither salty nor sweet. I skip the soup station, where the contents are often dark and unappealing, though there is a bean tuscan soup with mushrooms and rice that I accept. I skip the salad aisle—I do not like cold food. At the drinks station, I take orange juice. The lemonade has a brushed golden color and smells faintly of mint, but it is sweet beyond what seems necessary, more syrup than refreshment. Beside it stands the milk machine, a stainless steel container that rises nearly to my height.

At the lemongrass station, I take chopsticks. I hold them in my left hand even though I am right-handed, as a form of practice, and because it leaves my right hand free for my phone. The counter has rows of small sauce bottles, none taller than a palm. There are spring rolls sometimes, and a pink bun whose color seems to belong to something other than food. At the pizza section, cheese pizza appears perhaps four days out of seven. Once I saw the netting above the pizza fall onto it; when I told the staff, they put it back without changing the pizza beneath. I ate elsewhere that day.

I sit at the steel tables, each with a paper box of tissues, a white cup of salt, and a black cup of pepper. I eat for twenty or thirty minutes, sometimes returning for more. I watch people: couples, homeless men who clean their tables carefully and others who do not, classmates I half-recognize from lectures. One such classmate sat reading with his legs crossed in a posture I associated with old European paintings, drinking tea; we spoke, and later we became roommates. This is how relationships form in such a place—not through design, but through repeated presence.

The dining hall has run reliably all year, and I am grateful. It has kept me fed, and food is the foundation of everything else. But I cannot pretend that the food itself is good. There was a fish, served on a Tuesday, that made me sick for two days. The smell of it follows me like a warning, and I give that section of the counter a wide berth. This institution commands resources beyond what most can imagine and employs a great number of hardworking staff, yet a gap between effort and result remains that I have not seen explained. I do not say this to complain—I am a guest here—but to record what I have observed.

**Postscript**

I chose sustenance as my guiding principle because the entire essay is organized around eating, and because food is the most basic condition of any activity. A more abstract principle like truth or knowledge would have required me to step outside my subject, whereas sustenance was already present in everything I described. I omitted my academic life entirely—my classes, my grades, my intellectual development—because the dining hall is a space that exists apart from those things, and introducing them would have broken the frame. I also omitted my family, my friends outside Crossroads, and most of my background, because the assignment asked for a record of a single place and its routines, and a chronicle must stay within its chosen boundaries. Writing in this form revealed how much of a Mughal text is performance: the praise is real but partial, the critique is present but unstated, and the writer positions himself as a faithful recorder while carefully selecting what to include and what to leave out. The chronicler appears to be simply describing, but every description is a choice.
